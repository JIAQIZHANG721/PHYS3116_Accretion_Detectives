This week I looked more closely at three unusual clusters from the age–metallicity analysis: NGC 1851, Terzan 7 and Pal 12. I compared their ages, metallicities and age residuals with previous studies to see whether there is independent evidence for an accreted origin.

| Cluster | Age (Gyr) | [Fe/H] | Age residual | Literature evidence |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| NGC 1851 | 7.64 | -1.13 | -4.323 | Possible dwarf-galaxy origin |
| Terzan 7 | 7.40 | -0.12 | -3.455 | Associated with the Sagittarius dwarf galaxy |
| Pal 12 | 8.60 | -0.81 | -3.012 | Strong evidence for a Sagittarius origin |

NGC 1851 has the largest negative age residual in my analysis, so it appears younger than expected from the main age–metallicity trend. Previous studies have suggested that NGC 1851 may have formed in a dwarf galaxy that was later disrupted by the Milky Way. Its multiple stellar populations and unusual chemical properties also support this possibility. However, its exact origin is still uncertain, so I would treat it as a possible accreted candidate rather than a confirmed one.

Terzan 7 is also clearly younger than the main age–metallicity relation in my results. This agrees well with the literature, because Terzan 7 is strongly associated with the Sagittarius dwarf galaxy. Its relatively young age and high metallicity compared with many Milky Way globular clusters are consistent with a different chemical evolution history. This makes Terzan 7 a strong example of a cluster with an external origin.

Pal 12 also lies below the main age–metallicity trend and appears younger than expected. Previous studies strongly connect Pal 12 with the Sagittarius dwarf galaxy using its proper motion, orbit, chemical abundances and nearby Sagittarius stream stars. This agrees well with the result from my age–metallicity analysis and makes Pal 12 one of the strongest accreted candidates in this comparison.

Overall, the literature agrees quite well with the unusual clusters found in the age–metallicity analysis. Terzan 7 and Pal 12 have strong independent evidence linking them to the Sagittarius dwarf galaxy. NGC 1851 is less certain, but there are still studies suggesting that it may have formed in an accreted dwarf galaxy. This suggests that age–metallicity residuals can be useful for finding interesting candidates, but they are not enough on their own to confirm an accreted origin.

The age residual alone cannot show that a cluster was accreted. The fitted age–metallicity relation is only a simple linear model, and the measured ages and metallicities also have uncertainties. Different studies can also give slightly different values for the same cluster. For this reason, orbital, chemical and spatial evidence should also be considered before making a stronger conclusion.
